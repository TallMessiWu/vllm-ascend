The photographer had left no note about the people on the platform. On the back of the print there was only a date, a pencilled letter, and the impression of a paper clip. Nora looked for the roof in photographs of other stations, but most of the pictures had been taken from the street. This one showed the inside of the station, where timber columns divided the platform from the booking hall. The clock above the doorway had lost one hand. A woman in a dark coat was looking up at it while the people around her examined their tickets or watched the tracks.

The museum had inherited the print from a repair shop. It had been folded into thirds and stored with invoices for cameras that people had brought in decades earlier. Two creases had taken away narrow bands of detail. The first ran through the floor, where the pattern of tiles made a reasonable reconstruction possible. The other crossed the face of a man standing near the edge of the picture. Nora could restore the floor using neighbouring tiles, but no similar source existed for the man's expression. She decided to keep a version with the damage visible and make an exhibition copy with the smallest possible repair.

She scanned both sides at the same resolution, using a sheet of grey card to keep the pale borders distinct from the background. The first scan revealed another mark near the lower left corner. She thought it was a date until she turned the image. It was the number on a luggage label. The trunk bearing the label belonged to someone outside the frame, and the porter in the foreground had already put one hand on its handle. The label's typeface matched a printer whose surviving catalogue included several orders for station supplies.

The most promising clue came from a retired architect. He recognized the narrow stone band below the roof and remembered seeing it in an engineering report. The report described the station before a fire destroyed the western end. It also included a drawing of the internal columns, with the distance between them written in feet. Nora compared the intervals in the drawing with the intervals in the picture. Perspective made the two sets of measurements look different initially, but when she plotted them against their distance from the camera, their ratios agreed.

The tracking work changed the exhibition text. Instead of giving each unknown traveller a plausible story, Nora described the carbon copies, builders' drawings, and labels that made it possible to identify the location. She invited visitors to notice which evidence told them something about the place and which details remained open to interpretation. A rail timetable helped explain why several people were waiting at once, though a timetable could not tell her whether the train had been late or where the travellers intended to go.

On opening day, a visitor paused beside the damaged photograph and pointed to the woman under the clock. He recognized the pattern on the edge of her scarf, which his grandmother had knitted in alternating strips of wool. He had brought a second print from home. It showed the same woman facing the camera, standing beside a garden fence instead of a station wall. Nora asked him to turn the print over before they discussed the resemblance. The inscription gave an address and a name, but the date had been written by a different hand. It would take another conversation to decide whether the two pieces of evidence belonged together.

The north shore of the reservoir is shallow enough for reeds to grow well beyond the gravel path. After three weeks of warm weather, a monitoring team noticed fish gathering at the outlet before sunrise. The outlet carries moving water, and the movement could explain why the fish had gone there. The team also suspected a decline in dissolved oxygen elsewhere along the shore. An afternoon reading taken on the previous day had been normal, so a single visit during working hours was unlikely to distinguish between the explanations.

The team divided the shore into four sections and installed inexpensive sensors at the surface and near the bottom in each section. Each instrument recorded temperature and dissolved oxygen every ten minutes. A stream entering the reservoir on the eastern shore served as a comparison site. One person cleaned the sensors before installation, and another checked their readings together in a bucket of aerated water. The readings agreed closely enough that any large difference in the reservoir was unlikely to come from calibration alone. The instruments were labelled before the team loaded them into the boat.

On the first night the surface temperature fell slowly, and the water near the bottom remained warmer than expected. The dissolved oxygen readings began to separate after midnight. The largest drop occurred in the two bays with thick mats of floating plants. At dawn the outlet was still well oxygenated. A student suggested that the plants should have increased oxygen during the night, because green leaves were often mentioned in explanations of photosynthesis. The team plotted the readings against hours of daylight. Oxygen tended to rise whenever the plants had light and fall after dark, when plant cells and decomposing material continued to use oxygen.

A narrow channel connecting one bay to the main body of the reservoir had been partially blocked by fallen branches. When a short storm brought cool air and wind, the open sections mixed rapidly. Sensor readings from the isolated bay changed much less during the same interval. The wind event was useful evidence, but it did not establish the independent contribution of shade, water depth, or decomposition. The team used the next calm evening to measure the depth of each bay and collect small water samples at fixed positions in the channel.

When setting out options for the council, the researchers began with the observation that produced the most direct result. Removing the accumulated branches would increase water exchange through the channel. Cutting back all the floating plants would introduce several changes at once, including altered shade and suspended organic material. The team proposed opening a narrow passage first while leaving two nearby strips of vegetation undisturbed. Monitoring over another warm spell would show whether the daily oxygen minimum rose in the area connected by the passage.

The public sign at the reservoir was revised two days later. A simple drawing showed how readings at noon and before sunrise can differ even at the same location. Visitors asked whether a morning when no fish were visible would mean the work had succeeded. The staff explained that fish movement is also affected by weather, feeding, and disturbances in the water. A reliable conclusion would come from the instruments and from observations made repeatedly under comparable conditions. The next monitoring round would therefore keep the locations and sampling times fixed while the staff watched the channel for fresh debris.

The warehouse manager had to divide six deliveries between two loading bays on a day when one of the cranes would be inspected. One bay could accept tall vehicles, and the other had a low roof but a shorter route to the sorting room. The inspection would close the tall bay for forty minutes. A supplier delivering sealed drums had requested the first available slot because its driver would continue to another town. The carrier bringing empty containers was more flexible, though its vehicle was too tall to use the lower bay. Two refrigerated trucks could use either entrance if someone reserved a place near the cold room.

The initial schedule put the two refrigerated trucks side by side to make it easier for the same crew to unload them. The crew chief noticed that the cold room had space for one load until the outgoing delivery left. The second truck would have to wait with its refrigeration running, blocking the turn used by the service entrance. Moving that truck thirty minutes later gave the warehouse time to clear space. It also freed a loading bay for several short deliveries that could be completed without a crane.

The manager asked the planners to write their assumptions on the board before making another schedule. The stated arrival time of each truck was a promise to be available, not a guarantee that it would reach the door immediately. Unloading a tall truck was possible only at the tall bay, regardless of which equipment it used. A break in the schedule could be used for an inspection only when the crane was empty and the access gate was locked. The operations group agreed to treat these points as constraints and to estimate handling time separately from the advertised slot length.

By late morning a second complication appeared. A vehicle carrying boxed fittings arrived early, and the driver offered to use whichever entrance was available. Under the revised plan the low bay was open for eighteen minutes, which seemed adequate based on the packing list. When the warehouse clerk counted the boxes, she found several that were clearly marked for another department. Sorting them at the door would take roughly twice the usual time. The manager sent the vehicle to a waiting area and recorded the new estimate rather than trying to preserve the original timetable on paper.

After the inspection, both cranes were available until the lunch shift ended. The empty container truck could be taken first at the tall bay because it required little staging space. The early fittings delivery could enter the low bay as soon as a second clerk had time to check the labels. The planners chose this order even though it would leave one bay unused for eight minutes. Filling every minute of the schedule would otherwise have made the refrigerated load wait again, which was the more costly delay.

At the afternoon meeting the manager asked the crew to evaluate the plan against two figures. The first was the number of minutes the busiest vehicle waited after its agreed time. The second was the time during which no free position existed near the cold room. Average bay utilization was also recorded, but it was an incomplete picture on its own. A schedule could keep both cranes occupied and still delay the goods that were hardest to handle. The crew saved the revised table for the next inspection, with a note that a change in storage space would require a different order.

The community library was offered a grant to improve the reading room. The offer covered a single purchase this year and required the library to pay its own operating expenses after that. The board received proposals for brighter lighting, movable shelves, and a small room where visitors could take online lessons. Each proposal included a price for installation. The treasurer observed that the grant's headline amount made the three proposals look more similar than they were, because only one included a recurring software fee and another would require more staff time to maintain.

The library already paid for internet access, heating, and cleaning. Those costs were listed separately so the board could see which would actually change. Better lighting would use less power than the old fixtures, though the savings depended on how many hours the room stayed open. The movable shelves would be more expensive to buy and cheaper to maintain than the existing wooden units. The lesson room would need a managed computer, spare cables, and a way for visitors to book time without relying on a staff member at the desk. The grant would pay for the equipment and furnishings but not for the monthly licence.

A volunteer suggested ranking the proposals by the number of visitors likely to take advantage of them. The board sent a short survey to people who had used the library in the past year and kept paper copies near the entrance. Most responses came from regular visitors. Families with limited internet access were less likely to complete the online form, so the survey could not be interpreted as a complete estimate of demand for the lesson room. To give the board other evidence, the librarian counted requests for help with forms and online appointments during two normal weeks.

The planning group then built a three year budget. In its simplest version the budget used the advertised prices and assumed that use would stay close to present levels. In another version the library would open an extra evening each week. That scenario increased cleaning and lighting hours and made a private place for online lessons more valuable at the same time. The group kept both scenarios because the decision about evening opening would be made by a different committee. Combining them would obscure which recommendation depended on which operating schedule.

One contractor offered a discount if the library bought the lighting and shelves as a package. The offer looked attractive until the treasurer separated the work that would have to be changed when the room was enlarged. Several shelf units would block emergency access under the expansion drawing, and the lighting plan assumed a ceiling height that the new design no longer used. The board asked the contractor for separate prices and moved the expansion decision out of the current grant discussion. This kept a lower price from drawing the library into paying for work it might remove within a year.

The final funding proposal explained which costs were firm and which depended on use. It also reserved a small amount for the first year of replacement supplies so the opening month would not consume the ordinary book budget. Board members agreed to review participation after the first term, using visits and booked sessions rather than early survey responses alone. If demand arrived from visitors who had not answered the survey, the library could extend opening hours gradually and compare the benefit with the extra staffing cost before making a permanent change.
